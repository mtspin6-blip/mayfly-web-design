Montana mallard guide 2026–27

Plain-language summary of our research and data analysis. Full sourced version: mallard-map/docs/kb/EXPERT_GUIDE.md.

Using the map

  • Slide the week (or press ▶) to watch mallards build and move through the season. The color shows where they are that week.
  • Purple lines are the likely routes between areas. They get wider in weeks when lots of birds are moving.
  • Numbered pins are the 10 best spots for that week in the area you picked. Tap Best spots for the ranked list.
  • Zoom in on the satellite view to see the marshes, ponds and grain fields that make a spot good.

What mallards want

  • Food with energy: waste grain gives about 3x the usable energy of bugs, and barley is preferred over wheat. They also eat seeds and plants in shallow water.
  • Open water to roost on: big lakes, rivers, spring-fed water, and the stretches below dams that stay open.
  • Safety: under hunting pressure they sit on no-hunt water by day and feed at night.

Why and when they move

  • Day length gets them ready; weather makes them go. Shorter days prime birds to migrate, but weather is what actually starts the flight.
  • Mallards are tough. A bird in good shape can go about a week without food, and fat reserves let them winter in places as cold as North Dakota. They leave when ice and snow cut off food and water, not just because it's cold.
  • Snow is a big trigger: each extra centimeter of snow raised the chance of a further migration by about 80% in tracked Dakota mallards.
  • They fly mostly at night, 4,000–5,000 ft up, on a tailwind. One tracked mallard covered about 600 miles in a night.

What to watch for:

  1. A first hard freeze in Alberta or Saskatchewan sends new birds south into Montana.
  2. The night after the first low well below 14 °F with a north or northwest wind, expect fresh birds on open water south of the cold, and empty potholes where it froze.
  3. Once small water locks up (late October to early November on the prairie), birds pile onto rivers, tailwaters, spring creeks and big lakes.
  4. In warm, calm spells, birds stay put and just fly between roost and field.

Where and when in Montana

  • Early-season areas (Hi-Line, Great Falls): birds pass through in October and leave when the prairie water freezes.
  • Late-season areas (Flathead, Billings–Bighorn): open water keeps building birds into December and January.
  • Great Falls is in the Pacific Flyway (closes Jan 15). Early: Freezout Lake and Benton Lake. Late: the Missouri River through and below town.
  • Montana's fall mallards come mostly from Alberta and Saskatchewan. Birds now stop about 110 miles farther north than in the 1960s, and more of them head to Washington and Oregon.
  • 2026 breeding mallards were down 33% in the western Dakotas and Montana, so expect fewer local birds early. The late season depends on northern birds and weather.

A mallard's day

  • Roost on open water, then fly out to feed, usually 1–3 miles away and sometimes 12–20. Peak flights are at dawn and dusk.
  • More snow means longer, later feeding flights. In extreme cold they fly less.
  • Hunt the field lanes between big roosts and the nearest stubble, and give refuge closures a buffer.

Other ducks

  • Blue-winged teal leave early. Tracked Saskatchewan teal crossed Montana's south edge around Oct 12.
  • Diving ducks (scaup, redheads, goldeneyes) stay later on deep lakes like Fort Peck and Canyon Ferry, which freeze last.
  • Mallards are 41–65% of Montana's duck harvest; green-winged teal, wigeon and gadwall are a growing share.

How the map works

  • Color (heat map): eBird Status & Trends, Cornell Lab's model of weekly mallard numbers built from millions of birder checklists. Each area is about 6 miles wide.
  • Best spots: the strongest distinct areas in that model, at least 12 miles apart. The same lake is listed once. They're re-ranked for every week.
  • Flight paths: when eBird shows one area losing mallards and another gaining them in the same week, a route is drawn between them. Routes are steered by the directions real tracked birds flew: Dakota mallards went southeast or south 134 out of 147 times, and teal crossing this region averaged southeast. West of the Divide, routes lean toward Idaho, Washington and Oregon, where band recoveries show those birds winter. Crossing the Divide is made harder.
  • Beyond Montana (dashed lines, "Big picture" button): birds arrive from southern Alberta and southwest Saskatchewan, where band recoveries show most of the region's fall mallards come from. East of the Divide they head on toward Nebraska, Kansas and Colorado. Those destinations are placed using the real median first fall trip of tracked Dakota mallards: 838 km (520 mi) at 158°, south-southeast. West of the Divide they head to the Columbia Basin in Washington and Idaho's Snake River Plain, where band recoveries show Pacific-route mallards winter. These are broad directions, not exact routes.
  • Honest limits: no public GPS mallard tracks exist for Montana yet, so the paths are estimates, not recorded flights. Spots and timing: medium confidence. Weather rules: medium to high east of the Divide. Paths: low to medium.
