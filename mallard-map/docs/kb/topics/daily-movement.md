# A mallard's day and hunting pressure (REAL, published studies — S19, S20, S22)

- Roost → field: most wintering mallards fly 1–5 km between roost and food; some regularly 20–30 km; rare trips 60–100 km.
  Fewer wetlands around = longer flights (Mississippi Valley study). (S19)
- Field feeding: waste grain gives ~3x the usable energy of invertebrates; in Saskatchewan mallards preferred and depleted
  barley faster than wheat (intake rate drives choice). In Nebraska, snow cover changed how far and when birds flew to feed. (S22)
- Cold: in Tennessee, mallards flew less at low temperatures (except when hunting was closed); extreme cold can cut feeding flights. (S20)
- Pressure: in Arkansas and Tennessee, tracked mallards spent the day on closed refuges and fed on private land at night,
  avoided public hunting areas, and rarely hopped between refuges > 19 mi apart. In California, ~91% of mallard deaths
  were from hunting and higher pressure meant higher mortality. (S20)
- Migration flights are mostly at night, often 4,000–5,000 ft, and launch with tailwinds; a tracked mallard covered
  ~600 mi in 8 h on a strong tailwind. (S24)

What this means in Montana (inference, not tested here): hunt the fields or travel lanes within a few miles of big
open-water roosts (Flathead Lake north shore, Fort Peck, Bighorn, Missouri River, Canyon Ferry); expect birds to sit on
no-hunt water (refuge closed areas, town stretches of river) during heavy-pressure weekends; a big cold night with a
north wind can empty an area overnight — and fill one to the south.
